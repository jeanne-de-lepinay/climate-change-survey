Polar Expedition Survey

How do you feel about climate change?

This survey is conducted on HX expedition vessels to Antarctica & Arctic regions. It is an ongoing study on our ships' microcosm and will continue to evolve as more answers are added to the database. Results below represent the answers of guests who chose to participate — a self-selected group with an interest in science, nature, and polar environments.
Methodology — Surveys were distributed as paper forms and via QR code (online version) and transcribed by a single analyst (Jeanne de Lépinay). Where a respondent ticked two answers to a single-choice question, each was counted as 0.5. Unmarked scale items were excluded from that question's denominator. Participation bias, language barriers, question phrasing and single-analyst transcription may influence results.

Want to explore the science behind these questions?
The IPCC — Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change — is the United Nations body that synthesises peer-reviewed published climate science from thousands of independent researchers worldwide — assessing evidence, not advocating policy. Its conclusions account for the full span of Earth's climate history, including periods several millions of years ago when the planet was warmer, and for every known natural driver of variability: solar cycles, volcanic activity, orbital shifts. All have been scientifically measured and modelled and all data and methodologies are publicly available. The Sixth Assessment Report (AR6), published 2021–2023, is their most recent. The IPCC publishes a dedicated FAQ alongside every report — a good place to start if you want to do your own research scientifically.

Going deeper

Four respondent profiles emerge from the data
How these profiles were built
A k-means clustering (k=4, 100 initialisations) was applied to all 241 respondents with complete data across 8 variables: climate view, emotional reaction, outlook on action, societal change realism, willingness to act, personal worry, perceived public worry, and perceived guest worry.

Profiles are not pre-defined archetypes — they emerged from the data. Together they explain 50.8% of total variance (PC1: 32.7%, PC2: 18.1%).

What does "% serious believers" mean?
Q1 of the survey asked respondents to choose the statement that best matched their view of climate change. Respondents who selected "Climate change is a serious human-caused problem" are counted as "serious believers." A profile showing 0% means none of its members chose that answer — they instead selected views like "happening regardless of human action," "overstated," or "not happening."

A cluster analysis is a statistical method that groups respondents based on the similarity of their answers across all survey questions — without any prior assumptions about what those groups should look like. The algorithm identifies four natural profiles by finding patterns that minimise the difference within each group and maximise it between groups. Profiles are not mutually exclusive types: they represent regions of a continuous landscape. The clearest finding: nearly half of respondents (n=150) are high-belief, high-worry Committed Believers — the dividing line between them and the Strained Believers (n=76) is not belief intensity, but whether they still see collective change as possible.

Key findings

What this survey tells us

Conclusions are drawn from the complete dataset across all cruises.

Strong consensus — but not uniformity

With 362 respondents across three cruises, 68.5% see climate change as a serious human-caused problem and personal worry averages 7.49/10. The cluster analysis reveals the largest group (n=150, 47%) are Committed Believers — highly alarmed, hopeful and willing to act. A smaller but intense group (n=76 Strained Believers) shares equally high worry but skews overwhelmed or hopeless. The line between them is not about belief — it is about whether they still see change as possible.

The defining split: hope vs. exhaustion

Both the Committed Believer (n=150) and Strained Believer (n=76) profiles score above 8/10 on personal worry and include over 85% serious believers. What separates them is outlook: Committed Believers remain hopeful and willing to act (Q7 mean: 4.35/5), while Strained Believers skew overwhelmed, hopeless or skeptical (Q7 mean: 4.04/5). The Uncertain Moderate group (n=93) spans those uncertain about human causes alongside partial believers — still registering substantial personal worry (6.65/10) and openness to action, a nuanced position that resists simple "denier" framing.

The worry gap — a consistent three-tier structure

Respondents place their own personal concern at 7.49/10 — 2.72 points above where they rate the general public (4.77/10). Fellow guests are seen as more aware than the public (+1.68 points, 6.45/10), but still 1.04 points below respondents' own alarm. This three-tier structure is consistent across all three cruises and all 362 respondents. Political inaction is the top theme in open comments (28 mentions), with respondents highlighting governmental inaction and corporate priorities — suggesting the gap between personal alarm and collective response is increasingly felt as a political failure.
